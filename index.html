🚲 Bike Theft in NYC

Where bikes disappear — and what we can do about it

About This Project

This project explores NYC bike theft data collected by the NYPD. We aim to analyse the data provided with different visualization methods learned throughout the course. The result is this site, presenting the findings in interesting interactive ways. Using this public dataset, we identify temporal and spatial trends and visualize them across boroughs. We didn't ignore the human aspect of the crime, looking at the characteristics of the victims and suspects.

Use the menu to navigate to different page sections quickly: learn about the dataset, explore visualizations, and read our key findings.

Data Visualizations

But first a few visuals to describe what we are working with.

Dataset Overview

NYC Open Data from the NYPD with more then 80k theft cases, location, and time info: Visit Dataset .

Time Range

Data spans from 2006 to 2024.

City Coverage

All five boroughs: Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, Bronx, and Staten Island.

Regarding bike theft, there are three crime categories that the incident could be filed under: Grand Larceny, Petit Larceny, and Robbery. The distribution of our reported incidents between these categories can be seen below. As expected, in most cases, the cost of the bike fell under petit larceny.

For all these categories, the incident was unfortunately completed. The ratio between attempted and completed theft can be seen below. It is, however, natural that the attempted cases are overall low, as it is rare that someone reports a theft attempt. We thus conclude that all of the attempted cases were prevented by police intervention.

Who's affected: Sankey Diagram : Sex → Race → Age

The following Sankey diagrams illustrate the flow of crime data, separately for victims and suspects. Each chart visualizes how sex, race, and age group intersect within each group, highlighting the distribution and transitions between these attributes. Both Sankey charts reveal a predominantly male demographic, with the suspect chart showing an overwhelming majority of males. Young adults make up the largest age group in both charts, though suspects tend to be relatively younger. In terms of race, suspects are mostly identified as Black, while victims are predominantly White. This contrast may reflect underlying biases within the data. Notably, female victims appear more likely to report Black suspects compared to other racial groups, suggesting the presence of social or perceptual prejudice embedded in the reporting patterns.

Hovering over the links shows the actual number of people in the subcategory.

Trying to emphasize the relations between the two groups of people, we extended our analysis and added a correlation matrix. The observations we made about sex and age group are now more prominent. The correlation of the race descriptions is what gives us a better insight in the prejudice we assumed earlier.

Temporal Analysis

We move on to analysing the time of the incidents. The fastest way to get a good representation of the time distribution of the events is a bar plot over time. By choosing the time scale of the axis using the dropdown menu, one can see the crime distribution of the years registered The incidences appear constant throughout the days of the week or the month, but a clear pattern can be observed when looking at the distribution through hours of the day and months of the year. Surprisingly, the majority of reports happen in the late evening, leading us to the conclusion that the actual crime also took place around that time.

The relation between crime rates and the month of the year is also obvious and will be discussed below.

We chose to explore the relationship between incident rates and time by plotting their progression across months, this time broken down by borough. The resulting charts reveal a clear seasonal periodic pattern, suggesting that bike usage—and consequently, bike thefts—increase during the summer months. Another pleasant insight we get from the line graph is that the recent years we notice a substantial drop in cases, even during the summer months

One more observation is that the total reports in Manhattan and Brooklyn are consistently higher than the other boroughs. We elaborate on the special distributions of our data in the next section. In the meantime, explore the following graph and notice the pattern firsthand.

Neighbourhood safety

We continue our analysis of the data with our final visualization, a map through time. We already understood that the distribution of incidences is not smooth in all boroughs, with Manhattan and Brooklyn being the neighbourhoods with the majority of cases. In order to showcase this point further, we created a video of all the reports on the map of NYC. Every frame is an hour passing by. With the use of a heatmap, we make our point of easily spotting the hotspots in each neighbourhood.

Key Findings

After we analyzed different aspects of bike theft in NYC, we reached the following conclusions:

  • Most bike theft cases are reported after the fact, leaving the police departments unable to help.
  • Victims reporting the incidents are prejudiced, skewing the data
  • Summer months have higher incidents as people use their bikes more.
  • Although people have secure parking spaces for overnight storage, they often have a false sense of security when leaving their bikes parked outside during the day. Most thefts actually occur during daylight hours, particularly in the evening around 6 p.m..
  • Most thefts occur in Manhattan and Brooklyn
